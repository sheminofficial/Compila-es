# Habakkuk

Editorial manuscript for *The Bible, Retold — A Literary Adaptation for Young English Readers*. The Protestant World English Bible is the public-domain source. The prophet’s questions, God’s answers, five warnings, and final prayer are preserved without pretending that the book removes every mystery about suffering. This adaptation is intended to be read alongside Scripture.

## Chapter 1 — How Long?

Habakkuk’s revelation began not with an answer, but with a cry.

“How long, Lord?”

The prophet had called for help and seemed to hear only silence. He cried out about violence, yet rescue did not come. Everywhere he looked he saw wrongdoing, destruction, arguments, and conflict. The law appeared paralyzed. Justice could not reach its goal because wicked people surrounded the righteous, bending every decision until justice came out twisted.

God answered, but not in the way Habakkuk expected.

“Look among the nations,” the Lord said. “Watch and be amazed.” God was already doing something in Habakkuk’s days—something so astonishing that the prophet would struggle to believe it even after hearing it explained.

The Lord was raising the Chaldeans, the Babylonians: a fierce and restless nation that marched across the earth and seized homes that belonged to others. They inspired fear and made their own power the measure of justice and honor.

Their horses moved faster than leopards and attacked more fiercely than hungry wolves at dusk. Riders came from far away, rushing forward like an eagle diving toward prey. Their armies advanced for violence, gathering prisoners as easily as wind gathers grains of sand.

Kings were objects of mockery to them. They laughed at rulers and fortresses. When a city wall stood in their way, they piled up earth against it, climbed the ramp, and took the city. Then they swept onward like wind.

Their success did not make them innocent. They were guilty people who treated their own strength as a god.

The answer gave Habakkuk another question. The Lord was eternal—his God, his Holy One, his Rock. Habakkuk trusted that God had appointed Babylon as an instrument of judgment, not as the final ruler of history.

Yet how could the Holy One use a nation even more wicked than Judah? God’s eyes were too pure to approve evil. Why, then, did he tolerate betrayal? Why remain silent while the wicked swallowed people more righteous than themselves?

Human beings seemed like fish in the sea, with no ruler to protect them. Babylon caught them with hooks, swept them into nets, and dragged them away. The conqueror celebrated the catch. He honored his net as if the weapon itself were divine, because conquest provided him with rich food and a luxurious life.

Would he keep emptying that net only to cast it again? Would the nations continue to die without mercy?

The chapter ended there, with the question still open and the prophet waiting for God to speak again.

## Chapter 2 — Write the Vision Plainly

Habakkuk took his place like a guard. He stood at the watchtower and looked outward, waiting to learn how God would answer his complaint—and how he himself should respond.

The answer came with a command: write the vision clearly on tablets so that a person running with the message could read and carry it.

The vision belonged to an appointed time. It moved toward its fulfillment and would not prove false. If it seemed slow, Habakkuk was to wait. It would certainly come at the right time.

The proud person was swollen with self-importance and had no uprightness within. By contrast, the righteous person would live by faithfulness—by continuing to trust God.

Babylon’s appetite would never be satisfied. Like the grave and like death, the arrogant conqueror kept reaching for more, gathering nations and peoples into his power. But the conquered nations would one day raise a song of mockery against him.

Five warnings followed.

First: sorrow to the one who piles up what belongs to others and grows rich through debt and extortion. The victims would suddenly rise. The nation that made others tremble would itself become plunder. Because Babylon had robbed many peoples and shed human blood—bringing violence upon lands, cities, and inhabitants—the survivors would rob Babylon.

Second: sorrow to the one who builds a household through evil profit and sets his nest high, imagining no danger can reach it. By destroying many peoples, he planned shame for his own house and sinned against his own life. Even stones in the wall and wooden beams would cry out as witnesses.

Third: sorrow to the one who constructs a city with blood and establishes it through injustice. Nations exhaust themselves building what fire will consume. Their labor ends in emptiness. Yet the earth will not always be filled with the fame of violent empires. It will be filled with knowledge of the Lord’s glory as completely as water covers the sea.

Fourth: sorrow to the one who makes a neighbor drunk in order to expose and humiliate that person. The one who poured the cup would also have to drink. Shame, not glory, would cover him when the cup of judgment came from the Lord’s hand. Violence against Lebanon, the destruction of its animals, human bloodshed, and attacks on lands and cities would return upon the attacker.

Fifth: sorrow to the idol maker. What help could come from an image shaped by its own worshiper? A carved figure was a teacher of lies. Gold and silver might cover it, but no breath lived inside. Wood could not wake, and stone could not rise to teach.

The final contrast required no decoration.

The Lord was in his holy temple.

All the earth was to fall silent before him.

## Chapter 3 — Yet I Will Rejoice

Habakkuk’s last chapter was a prayer prepared for music.

He had heard of the Lord’s fame and stood in awe of what God had done. He asked God to renew his work in their own years and make it known again. Even in wrath, the prophet prayed, remember mercy.

The song pictured God coming from Teman and the Holy One from Mount Paran. His glory spread across the heavens, while praise filled the earth. Splendor shone like sunrise. Rays flashed from his hand, though the fullness of his power remained hidden there.

Plague went ahead of him and pestilence followed. When he stood, the earth shook. One look made nations tremble. Mountains that seemed ancient crumbled, and age-old hills sank down. God’s ways were older and more lasting than they were.

Habakkuk saw the tents of Cushan in distress and the dwellings of Midian trembling.

Was God angry with the rivers? Was his wrath directed at the sea? No—the waters in the poem became the road for his horses and his chariots of salvation. He uncovered his bow and called for arrows according to his oath. Rivers split the earth. Mountains saw him and shook; storm waters rushed past; the deep roared and lifted its waves like hands.

Sun and moon appeared to stand still while arrows flashed and a spear glittered. God marched through the land in anger and threshed the nations like grain.

Yet his purpose was rescue. He went out for the salvation of his people and of his anointed one. He crushed the head of the wicked power and stripped its rule from top to bottom. Enemy warriors who came like a whirlwind to scatter the weak were pierced with their own weapons. God’s horses moved through the sea, churning the mighty waters.

The vision did not leave Habakkuk feeling powerful. His body trembled when he heard it. His lips shook. Weakness seemed to enter his bones, and his legs could barely hold him. He had to wait quietly for the day of trouble, when the invading people would come.

Faith did not make the approaching loss imaginary.

The fig tree might produce no blossoms. Vines might hold no grapes. Olive crops might fail, and fields might yield no food. Sheep could disappear from their pens and cattle from their stalls. Every familiar sign of nourishment and security might be gone.

“Yet,” Habakkuk declared, “I will rejoice in the Lord.”

He would find joy in the God who saved him. The Lord God was his strength. God could give him feet as sure as a deer’s and enable him to walk across dangerous heights without falling.

The prayer closed with an instruction for the music director: it was to be played on stringed instruments. Habakkuk’s questions had not been erased. The invasion had not been canceled. But the complaint that began with “How long?” had become a song that could face an empty field and still trust God.
