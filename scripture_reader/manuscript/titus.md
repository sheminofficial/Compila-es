# Titus

Editorial manuscript for *The Bible, Retold — A Literary Adaptation for Young English Readers*. The Protestant World English Bible is the public-domain source. Paul’s instructions are preserved in their first-century church and household setting, including difficult language about Crete and enslaved servants, without turning ancient social conditions into timeless approval. This adaptation is intended to be read alongside Scripture.

## Chapter 1 — Put What Remains in Order

Paul introduced himself as God’s servant and an apostle of Jesus Christ. His work served the faith of God’s chosen people and the knowledge of truth that produces a godly life. That work rested on the hope of eternal life—life promised before time began by the God who does not lie.

At the proper time, God had made his word known through the message entrusted to Paul by the command of God their Savior.

Paul wrote to Titus, his true child in the faith they shared. He asked that grace, mercy, and peace would come to him from God the Father and from Jesus Christ their Savior.

Titus had been left on the island of Crete with unfinished work. Churches had begun, but they needed order and trustworthy leadership. In every city he was to appoint elders according to the directions Paul had given him.

An elder’s public responsibility had to be supported by a faithful life. He should be above accusation, faithful in marriage, and the head of a household not known for reckless or rebellious behavior. An overseer managed something belonging to God, not to himself.

For that reason, he could not be arrogant, quick-tempered, controlled by wine, violent, or hungry for dishonest profit. His life should show the opposite qualities: hospitality, love for what is good, sound judgment, justice, holiness, and self-control.

He also had to hold firmly to the trustworthy message he had been taught. Only then could he encourage the church through healthy teaching and answer those who opposed it.

Crete’s churches faced many disorderly talkers and deceivers, especially among teachers who insisted on circumcision. Their claims were disrupting entire households, and money drove some of their teaching. Titus could not leave the harm unanswered.

Paul quoted a harsh line from a Cretan voice who had described his own people as constant liars, dangerous animals, and lazy gluttons. Paul said the testimony fit the false teachers he was confronting and told Titus to correct them sharply. The purpose of correction was not humiliation for its own sake; it was that they might become healthy in faith.

They must stop chasing Jewish myths and human commands created by people who had turned away from truth.

Purity could not be produced by endless suspicion about ordinary things. To the pure, Paul said, all things are pure. But when mind and conscience are corrupted by unbelief, nothing is received in purity.

The deceivers claimed to know God, yet their actions denied him. Their confession and conduct moved in opposite directions. They were disobedient and unprepared for any genuinely good work.

That contrast explained the kind of leaders Crete needed. Impressive speech was not enough. A steward of God’s household had to teach truth with a life that did not contradict it.

## Chapter 2 — Grace That Teaches Us to Live

Titus was to teach what belonged with healthy doctrine—not ideas that remained in the air, but truth visible in ordinary lives.

Older men were to be temperate, worthy of respect, and sound in judgment. Their faith, love, and perseverance should remain strong.

Older women were likewise to behave with reverence. They must not destroy others with slander or become enslaved to much wine. Instead, they were to teach what was good. Through their example and guidance, younger wives could learn faithful love for husbands and children, self-control, purity, care for their homes, kindness, and respect within marriage, so that God’s word would not be dishonored through their conduct.

Younger men received a short command with a wide reach: be self-controlled.

Titus himself could not teach these things from a safe distance. He was to become an example of good works. His teaching needed integrity, seriousness, and sound speech that could survive examination. An opponent might still disagree, but should have no true evil to report about the believers.

Paul also addressed enslaved servants living inside the Roman household system. They were to respect their masters, seek to please them, avoid defiant argument and theft, and show complete faithfulness. Even people with little social power could make the teaching about God their Savior attractive through trustworthy conduct. The passage instructed believers within that existing system; it did not call exploitation good.

Then Paul gave the reason that joined every group together.

The grace of God had appeared, bringing salvation for all people. Grace did more than pardon the past. It trained believers to reject ungodliness and desires shaped by the present world. It taught them to live with self-control, justice, and devotion to God in the present age.

They lived while looking forward—waiting for the blessed hope and the appearing glory of their great God and Savior, Jesus Christ.

Jesus had given himself for them. He came to redeem them from lawlessness and purify a people who belonged especially to him, a people eager to do good.

Titus was to teach, encourage, and correct with the authority of that message. He was not to let others dismiss him.

The chapter’s commands differed with age, responsibility, and social position, but their center was the same. God’s grace had appeared. That grace formed a people whose faith could be seen in patience, truth, kindness, self-control, and good work while they waited for Christ.

## Chapter 3 — Saved by Mercy, Ready for Good Work

Titus was to remind the believers how to live in public. They should respect rulers and authorities, obey, and remain prepared for every good work. They must not slander anyone or become eager for conflict. Gentleness and humility were to shape their behavior toward all people.

There was no room for superiority.

“We too were once foolish,” Paul wrote. Believers had known disobedience and deception. Desires and pleasures had ruled them. Malice and envy filled their relationships until people became hateful and hated one another.

Then the kindness of God their Savior appeared, along with his love for humanity.

God did not save them because they had built an impressive record of righteous actions. He saved them according to his mercy. Paul described this rescue as a washing of new birth and a renewal by the Holy Spirit. God poured out the Spirit generously through Jesus Christ their Savior.

Grace declared them right and made them heirs—people who could confidently hope for eternal life.

Paul called this message trustworthy. Titus should insist on it, so that those who believed God would devote careful attention to good works. Good works did not purchase salvation; they grew from mercy and brought real benefit to other people.

Not every religious discussion produced that kind of fruit. Titus was to avoid foolish controversies, arguments over genealogies, quarrels, and fights about the law. Such disputes were empty and unhelpful.

A person who repeatedly created factions was to receive a first warning and then a second. If the divisive behavior continued, the church was to step away. Persistent division showed that the person’s direction had become twisted and sinful; his own conduct judged him.

The letter ended with travel plans and practical needs.

Paul would send either Artemas or Tychicus to relieve Titus. When one of them arrived, Titus should make every effort to join Paul at Nicopolis, where Paul had decided to spend the winter.

Zenas, a lawyer, and Apollos were also traveling. Titus was to help them quickly and make sure they lacked nothing for the journey.

The churches had to learn this pattern too. Their good works should meet urgent needs so that their lives would not remain unfruitful.

Everyone with Paul sent greetings. Titus was to greet the friends who loved them in the faith.

“Grace be with you all.”

The letter had begun with unfinished churches and ended with believers learning to notice what others needed. Between those points stood the kindness of God: mercy that saved people who could not save themselves, then taught them to become ready for every good work.
