# Zephaniah

Editorial manuscript for *The Bible, Retold — A Literary Adaptation for Young English Readers*. The Protestant World English Bible is the public-domain source. The book’s severe judgment poetry, geographical names, calls to humility, and promises of restoration are preserved without assigning its “day of the Lord” imagery to a modern event. This adaptation is intended to be read alongside Scripture.

## Chapter 1 — The Day Drawing Near

The word of the Lord came to Zephaniah during the reign of Josiah son of Amon, king of Judah. The prophet was introduced through four generations: Zephaniah son of Cushi, son of Gedaliah, son of Amariah, son of Hezekiah.

His opening vision was vast and frightening. The Lord announced a sweeping judgment over the surface of the land: people and animals, birds and fish, ruins and the wicked would all be caught within it.

Then the vision narrowed to Judah and Jerusalem.

God would stretch out his hand against the city’s mixture of worship and unfaithfulness. The remains of Baal worship would be removed, along with the names of idolatrous priests. Some people bowed to the stars from their rooftops. Others claimed loyalty to the Lord while also swearing by Malcam. Still others had simply turned away and no longer sought the Lord or asked for his guidance.

“Be silent before the Lord God,” Zephaniah warned. “The day of the Lord is near.”

The prophet pictured the day as a sacrifice already prepared, with guests set apart. Princes, royal sons, and those displaying foreign clothing would face examination. So would people associated with leaping over the threshold and filling their master’s house with violence and deceit.

Cries would rise across Jerusalem: from the Fish Gate, from the Second Quarter, and from the hills. The business district of Maktesh would mourn. Its merchants would be ruined, and those weighed down with silver would be cut off.

No dark corner would hide indifference. God would search Jerusalem with lamps. He would find people settled comfortably like wine left on its sediment, telling themselves that the Lord would do neither good nor harm. Their belief that God would never act had allowed them to live without concern.

Their wealth would become plunder and their houses ruins. They would build homes but not live in them, plant vineyards but never taste the wine.

The great day was not drifting slowly toward them. It was near and hurrying. Even a warrior would cry bitterly when it arrived.

Zephaniah gathered phrase after phrase: a day of wrath, distress, anguish, trouble, ruin, darkness, gloom, cloud, and deep shadow. Trumpets would sound alarms against fortified cities and high towers. People would move as though blind because they had sinned against the Lord. The poem used brutal images of blood and bodies to show how completely human strength would fail.

Silver and gold could not buy rescue from divine judgment. Wealth, status, walls, and soldiers offered no shield. The land would be consumed by the fire of God’s jealousy, and its inhabitants would face a sudden, terrible end.

The chapter did not invite readers to enjoy destruction. It tore away the illusion that divided worship, injustice, and comfortable unbelief could continue forever without an answer.

## Chapter 2 — Seek Humility

Before the appointed day passed like chaff in the wind, Zephaniah called the nation to gather. There was still time to listen before the Lord’s fierce anger arrived.

“Seek the Lord,” he told the humble people who had obeyed God’s commands. “Seek righteousness. Seek humility.” Perhaps they would be sheltered in the day of anger.

The prophet then looked west toward the Philistine coast. Gaza would be abandoned, Ashkelon reduced to ruins, Ashdod emptied at midday, and Ekron pulled up like a plant from the ground. The Cherethites and the land of the Philistines stood beneath the Lord’s word.

Cities would give way to pasture. Shepherds’ shelters and sheepfolds would line the coast. The surviving people of Judah would graze their flocks there and rest in Ashkelon’s houses at evening. The Lord their God would visit them and restore their fortunes.

Zephaniah turned east. God had heard Moab’s contempt and Ammon’s insults against his people. Those nations had spoken proudly and threatened Israel’s borders. Therefore Moab would become like Sodom and Ammon like Gomorrah—a place of nettles, salt pits, and lasting desolation. The remnant of Judah would take possession of what remained.

Pride lay beneath their judgment. They had magnified themselves against the people of the Lord of Armies. But the Lord would show himself greater than the gods of the lands. People from distant coastlands would worship him, each from their own place.

The message reached south toward Cush: its people too stood under the sword of judgment.

Finally the vision moved north. God would stretch out his hand against Assyria and make Nineveh dry and empty like a wilderness. Herds and wild creatures would lie down where crowds had once lived. Pelicans and porcupines would shelter among ruined columns. Their cries would echo through open windows, and exposed cedar beams would show where rich ceilings had collapsed.

This had been a joyful, careless city, secure enough to say in its heart, “I am, and there is no one besides me.”

Now animals would rest there. Travelers would hiss and shake their fists at the ruins.

From Gaza to Nineveh, the same lesson crossed the map: no city, market, alliance, or god could make pride permanent. Yet the coast emptied by judgment would also become a resting place for a restored remnant. The call still stood between warning and ruin: seek the Lord, seek righteousness, seek humility.

## Chapter 3 — He Will Rejoice over You

Zephaniah returned his attention to the rebellious, polluted, and oppressive city of Jerusalem.

She had refused to obey God’s voice or accept correction. She had not trusted the Lord or drawn near to him. Her leaders acted like roaring lions, and her judges like evening wolves that devoured everything before morning. Her prophets were reckless and treacherous. Her priests treated the sanctuary as ordinary and twisted the law they were meant to guard.

Yet the Lord remained righteous within the city. He did no wrong. Morning after morning he brought justice into the light. He never failed, even when unjust people felt no shame.

Other nations had already fallen. Towers, streets, and cities stood deserted. God had intended those ruins to warn Jerusalem: fear him and receive correction, so that the city would not suffer the judgment appointed for it. Instead, the people rose early and hurried into further corruption.

Therefore they would have to wait for the day when God gathered nations and kingdoms for judgment. The fire of his jealousy would consume the earth.

But judgment was not the final movement of the prophecy.

God would purify the speech of peoples so that they could call on the Lord’s name and serve him shoulder to shoulder. Worshipers would come even from beyond the rivers of Cush, and dispersed people would bring offerings.

Jerusalem’s shameful rebellion would not define her forever. God would remove those who celebrated their pride. Arrogance would no longer rule his holy mountain. He would leave a humble and lowly people who found refuge in his name.

This remnant would not practice injustice or fill its speech with lies. No deceitful tongue would be found among them. They would feed and rest without anyone making them afraid.

The poem changed into a song.

“Sing, daughter of Zion. Shout, Israel. Rejoice with all your heart, daughter of Jerusalem.”

The Lord had removed the judgments against her and turned away her enemy. The Lord himself, the King of Israel, was among his people. They no longer needed to live in fear of disaster.

Jerusalem would hear another message: do not be afraid. Do not let your hands fall weak.

The Lord their God was among them, a mighty savior. He would rejoice over them with gladness. He would quiet them in his love and celebrate over them with singing.

Those who grieved because they could not join the appointed festivals would be gathered. God would deal with their oppressors. He would save the lame and bring home those driven away. People whose shame had spread across the earth would receive honor and praise.

“At that time I will bring you in,” the Lord promised. “At that time I will gather you.”

Their restoration would not happen far beyond their sight. God would restore their fortunes before their own eyes and give them honor among the peoples of the earth.

The book that began with everything being swept away ended with scattered people being gathered. The God whose justice exposed the city’s corruption would be present among a humbled people—not only ruling them, but rejoicing over them with a song.
