# Haggai

Editorial manuscript for *The Bible, Retold — A Literary Adaptation for Young English Readers*. The Protestant World English Bible is the public-domain source. Dates, named leaders, prophetic speech, and legal questions are preserved. This adaptation is intended to be read alongside Scripture.

## Chapter 1 — Consider Your Ways

It was the second year of King Darius, on the first day of the sixth month. The returned exiles lived again in Judah, but the Lord’s house still lay in ruins.

The word of the Lord came through Haggai the prophet to two leaders: Zerubbabel son of Shealtiel, the governor of Judah, and Joshua son of Jehozadak, the high priest.

The people had an explanation for the unfinished temple. “The time has not yet come,” they said. The right season to rebuild the Lord’s house must still be somewhere in the future.

God’s answer exposed the comfort hidden inside their delay. Was this the proper time for them to live in finished, paneled houses while his house remained a ruin?

“Consider your ways,” the Lord of Armies told them.

Their daily effort produced strangely little. They planted much but harvested little. They ate without feeling satisfied and drank without having enough. Clothes failed to keep them warm. A worker collected wages only to discover that his money seemed to fall through a bag full of holes.

Again came the command: consider your ways.

The people were to climb into the mountains, bring back timber, and build the house. God would take pleasure in it and be honored there.

They had expected much from their labor, but the results had been small. Even what they carried home did not last. The Lord explained why: his house lay deserted while everyone hurried to care for a private house. Therefore the sky had held back its dew and the earth its produce. Drought had reached the fields and hills, the grain, new wine, olive oil, every crop, the people, the livestock, and the work of their hands.

This time, the warning did not meet another excuse.

Zerubbabel, Joshua, and the whole remnant listened to the voice of the Lord their God. They recognized that Haggai had been sent by him, and they responded with reverence.

Then Haggai brought a shorter message—only a few words, but enough to change the weight of the task.

“I am with you,” the Lord said.

God stirred the spirit of the governor, the high priest, and all the remaining people. They came together and began work on the house of the Lord of Armies, their God.

The date was recorded: the twenty-fourth day of the sixth month, still in the second year of Darius. Twenty-three days had passed since Haggai’s first message. The temple was not yet finished, but delay had become obedience, and the work had begun.

## Chapter 2 — From This Day Forward

On the twenty-first day of the seventh month, the Lord spoke again through Haggai. The message went to Zerubbabel, Joshua, and everyone who remained among the people.

Some of the older people could remember the former temple and its glory. Beside that memory, the structure rising in front of them appeared to be nothing. The comparison could drain the strength from their hands.

But the Lord called each group to courage.

Be strong, Zerubbabel. Be strong, Joshua the high priest. Be strong, all you people of the land—and work.

The reason was not the size of their resources or the beauty already visible. “I am with you,” the Lord of Armies said. His covenant from the days when Israel came out of Egypt still stood, and his Spirit remained among them. They did not need to be afraid.

In a little while, God said, he would shake the heavens and earth, sea and dry land. The nations would be shaken, their treasures would come, and God would fill this house with glory. Silver and gold already belonged to him. The later glory of the house would be greater than the former glory, and in that place he would give peace.

Another message came on the twenty-fourth day of the ninth month. This time Haggai questioned the priests about the law.

If a person carried holy meat in the fold of a garment, and that cloth touched bread, stew, wine, oil, or some other food, would the food become holy?

The priests answered no.

If someone made unclean through contact with a dead body touched the food, would it become unclean?

Yes, they replied.

Haggai applied their answers to the nation. Holiness did not spread automatically from the temple work to everything the people did. Their uncleanness affected their labor and offerings. Building on sacred ground could not replace a changed relationship with God.

They were to look back carefully. Before one temple stone had been placed upon another, they had gone to a heap expecting twenty measures and found only ten. They had approached a wine vat expecting fifty and drawn only twenty. Blight, mildew, and hail had damaged the work of their hands, yet they had not turned back to God.

Now they were to mark the date: the twenty-fourth day of the ninth month, the day connected with laying the temple’s foundation. Seed still waited in the barn. Vine, fig tree, pomegranate, and olive had not yet produced their fruit. Nothing visible in the fields proved that the future would be different.

Still, the Lord declared, “From this day I will bless you.”

A second message came to Haggai that same day, directed to Zerubbabel. God would shake heaven and earth, overturn royal thrones, and break the power of foreign kingdoms. Chariots, horses, and riders would fall as armies turned their swords against one another.

In that day, God would take Zerubbabel son of Shealtiel, his chosen servant, and make him like a signet ring—the royal seal a king kept close and used to mark his authority.

The small community remained under a foreign emperor, working among stones and fields that had not yet yielded a harvest. Yet the final word did not belong to ruined walls or powerful kingdoms. It belonged to the Lord who was with them, who called them to work, and who promised blessing from that day forward.
