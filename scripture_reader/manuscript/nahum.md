# Nahum

Editorial manuscript for *The Bible, Retold — A Literary Adaptation for Young English Readers*. The Protestant World English Bible is the public-domain source. Nahum’s severe poetic images of judgment are preserved as prophecy against ancient Nineveh, without turning them into instructions for violence. This adaptation is intended to be read alongside Scripture.

## Chapter 1 — A Stronghold in the Storm

Nahum the Elkoshite received a vision concerning Nineveh, capital of the Assyrian Empire. Assyria’s strength had terrified nations, but this book began by looking beyond the empire to the character and power of the Lord.

The Lord is jealous for what is right, Nahum declared, and he takes vengeance against his enemies. His anger is not quick or careless: he is slow to anger and great in power. Yet patience does not mean that guilt will be ignored forever.

A storm became the road beneath his feet. Whirlwind and cloud moved like signs of his approach, the clouds no more than dust rising behind him. At his rebuke the sea dried, and rivers lost their water. The rich lands of Bashan and Carmel withered; Lebanon’s flowers faded. Mountains shook, hills seemed to melt, and the earth trembled with everyone who lived upon it.

Who could remain standing before such indignation? His wrath poured like fire, breaking rocks apart.

Then, inside the storm of judgment, Nahum spoke a sentence of shelter: the Lord is good. He is a stronghold in a day of trouble, and he knows those who take refuge in him.

The same flood that pictured an end to Nineveh could not wash away those words. God would pursue his enemies into darkness, but he recognized those who trusted him.

Nineveh’s plots against the Lord would fail. The suffering it had caused would not need to be defeated twice. Its people might be tangled together like thorns and soaked with drink, but they would burn like dry straw. From the city had come a counselor of wickedness who planned evil against the Lord.

The empire was numerous and appeared to possess its full strength. Even so, God said, it would be cut down and pass away.

Judah had also known affliction, but the Lord promised not to continue it. He would break Assyria’s yoke from Judah’s neck and tear apart the ropes that held the people down.

Against Nineveh came another command: its ruling name would not continue through descendants. Carved and metal images would be removed from the temples of its gods. The proud city that had prepared graves for others would receive a grave of its own.

But across Judah’s mountains came the feet of a messenger bringing good news and announcing peace.

Judah could once again celebrate its appointed feasts and fulfill its promises to God. The wicked invader would no longer march through the land. His power was cut off.

## Chapter 2 — The Lion’s Den Emptied

An attacker was advancing against Nineveh.

“Guard the fortress,” the prophet called. “Watch the road. Prepare yourselves. Gather all your strength.”

The warning sounded almost like military advice, but the chapter had already announced the outcome. The Lord was restoring the dignity of Jacob and Israel after destroyers had stripped their vines bare.

The assault flashed red and scarlet. Shields caught the color of battle. Chariots shone like steel and raced through the streets, appearing like torches and moving like lightning. Officers called their chosen troops, but soldiers stumbled as they hurried. They rushed toward the wall and raised a protective covering for the attack.

Then the river gates opened, and the palace collapsed into panic. The city’s fate had been decided. Nineveh was exposed and carried away. Servant women moaned like doves and beat their breasts in grief.

For generations the city had seemed like a pool filled with people and wealth. Now its inhabitants poured away like escaping water. Voices shouted, “Stop! Stop!” No one turned around.

The attackers took silver and gold. Nineveh’s treasure appeared endless, room after room filled with precious objects gathered by the empire. Soon the city was empty, stripped, and ruined. Hearts melted with fear. Knees knocked together, bodies trembled, and faces lost their color.

Nahum asked where the lions had gone.

Assyria had behaved like a den where lions walked without fear. Its rulers had torn other nations apart, carrying prey home to feed their young and filling their caves with what they had killed. The image that once displayed power now exposed the source of the empire’s wealth.

“I am against you,” said the Lord of Armies.

He would burn Nineveh’s chariots until smoke covered them. The sword would consume the young lions. Assyria would no longer hunt across the earth, and the voices of its messengers—those who carried commands and threats to other lands—would fall silent.

The lion’s den had been filled through violence. Now it stood empty.

## Chapter 3 — The City No One Mourned

Sorrow was pronounced over the city of blood. Nineveh was full of lies, robbery, and prey taken from others. Its violence seemed never to end.

Nahum made the battle audible: the crack of whips, wheels rattling, horses leaping forward, chariots bounding over the ground. Riders charged. Swords flashed and spearheads glittered. Then the motion ended in a terrible field of bodies—so many that people stumbled over the dead.

The prophet described Nineveh as an alluring prostitute and a mistress of sorcery, selling nations and families through deception. This was an image for the empire’s corrupting power, political seduction, and control.

Again the Lord of Armies declared, “I am against you.”

The city that had humiliated others would be publicly humiliated. Its shame would be uncovered before the nations. Filth would be thrown upon it, and its former admirers would turn away. People would see the waste and ask who could mourn for Nineveh. No comforters would come.

Nineveh was not the first great city to imagine its defenses could not fail. Nahum pointed to No-Amon, a powerful Egyptian city surrounded and protected by waters. Cush and Egypt had supplied enormous strength; Put and Libya had been allies. Still, No-Amon was carried into captivity. Its children were killed at the street corners, its honored leaders were divided by lot, and its great men were chained.

Nineveh would also stagger as though drunk and try to hide. It too would search for a refuge from the enemy.

Its fortresses would fall as easily as ripe figs shaken into an open mouth. The city’s defenders would be unable to stop the gates from opening, and fire would consume the bars.

The prophet spoke with bitter urgency: collect water for the siege, strengthen the walls, step into the clay, work the mortar, and prepare more bricks. Let the merchants multiply like grasshoppers and the soldiers like locusts. Let every possible defense gather.

Fire and sword would still reach the city.

Nineveh’s merchants were more numerous than the stars, yet they would strip what they could and fly away like insects. Guards and officials would resemble locusts resting on a wall in the cold. As soon as the sun warmed them, they would vanish, leaving no trace of where they had gone.

The king of Assyria would find his shepherds sleeping and his nobles lying still. The people would be scattered across the mountains with no one to gather them.

There was no medicine for the wound, Nahum said. The injury was fatal.

When the report traveled outward, other nations would clap—not because suffering itself was good, but because Assyria’s endless cruelty had touched them all. The empire had made victims everywhere. At last, the power that seemed permanent could harm them no more.
