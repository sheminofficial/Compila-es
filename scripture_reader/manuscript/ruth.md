# Ruth

Editorial manuscript for *The Bible, Retold — A Literary Adaptation for Young English Readers*. The Protestant World English Bible is the public-domain source. Dialogue below is adapted. This literary edition is intended to be read alongside Scripture.

## Chapter 1 — The Road Back

In the days when judges led Israel, a famine reached Bethlehem in Judah. Elimelech left the town with his wife, Naomi, and their two sons, Mahlon and Chilion. The family belonged to Bethlehem in Ephrathah, but for a time their home would be across the border, in Moab.

Elimelech died there. Naomi still had her sons, and the sons married Moabite women named Orpah and Ruth. About ten years passed in Moab. Then both sons died. The household that had come out of Bethlehem was down to Naomi, and the women her sons had married were widows too.

Word reached Naomi that the Lord had given his people food again. She set out for Judah, and both women left with her. On the road, Naomi asked them to turn back.

“Go home to your mothers,” she said. “May the Lord be as kind to you as you have been to me and to those who died. May he give each of you a home where you can live in peace with a husband.”

She kissed them. They all wept, and the two younger women insisted they would go with her to her people.

“Why come with me?” Naomi asked. “Do I have any more sons for you to marry? I’m too old to marry again. Even if I found a husband tonight and had sons, would you wait for them to grow up? Would you refuse to marry anyone else all that time? No, my daughters. I’m deeply grieved for you. The Lord’s hand has turned against me.”

They wept again. Orpah kissed Naomi goodbye. Ruth held on to her.

“Your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and her god,” Naomi said. “Go with her.”

“Please stop asking me to leave you. Wherever you go, I’ll go. Wherever you make your home, I’ll make mine. Your people will be my people, and your God will be my God. I’ll die where you die, and I’ll be buried there. May the Lord punish me severely if anything other than death separates us.”

Naomi saw that Ruth had made up her mind. She let the matter drop, and the two walked on to Bethlehem.

Their arrival stirred the whole town. “Is this Naomi?” the women asked.

“Call me Mara,” she told them. Naomi meant pleasant. Mara meant bitter. “The Almighty has made my life bitter. I left with a full household, and the Lord has brought me back empty. Why call me Naomi when the Lord has spoken against me and the Almighty has brought this suffering on me?”

That was how Naomi came back, with Ruth of Moab beside her. The barley harvest was just beginning.

## Chapter 2 — What the Harvesters Left

“Let me go to the fields,” Ruth said. “Someone may let me follow the harvesters and glean what they leave behind.”

“Go, my daughter,” Naomi said.

Ruth went out and found a place among the people gathering leftover grain. The part of the field where she happened to stop belonged to Boaz, a wealthy and respected man from Elimelech’s family.

When Boaz came from Bethlehem, he greeted the workers with a blessing. They called back, asking the Lord to bless him too. Then he turned to the man in charge of the harvesters and asked whose household the young woman belonged to.

“She’s the Moabite woman who came back with Naomi. She asked to pick up grain behind the workers, among the bundles. She’s been here since morning, apart from a short rest under shelter.”

Boaz went to speak to her. “Stay in this field, my daughter. Keep close to the women who work for me. Watch where they’re cutting and follow them. I’ve told the men to leave you alone. If you get thirsty, help yourself to the water they’ve drawn.”

Ruth bowed with her face to the ground. “Why would you pay attention to me and show me this kindness? I’m a foreigner.”

“I’ve heard what you did for Naomi after your husband died,” Boaz said. “You left your father and mother and the place where you were born. You came to live among people you had never known. May the Lord, the God of Israel, reward you in full for what you’ve done. You’ve come to take refuge under his wings.”

Ruth told him his words had comforted her, though she had no standing among his workers.

When it was time to eat, Boaz called her over. He offered her bread to dip in vinegar, and she sat down beside the harvesters. They passed her roasted grain. She ate her fill and saved what was left.

As soon as she got up to work, Boaz spoke to his men. They were to let her gather even among the bundles, where the grain had already been collected. They were to pull out stalks and leave them in her path. No one was to scold her for picking them up.

Ruth stayed until evening. When she beat the grain from the stalks she had gathered, she had about an ephah of barley. She carried it back into town. Naomi saw the barley, and Ruth handed her the food she had saved from the meal.

“Where did you work today?” Naomi asked. “May the man who noticed you be blessed!”

“His name is Boaz.”

“May the Lord bless him! He has kept showing kindness to the living and to the dead.” Then Naomi told Ruth who Boaz was: a close relative with a duty to help protect the family.

Ruth said he had told her to stay with his workers until the harvest was over. Naomi urged her to keep close to his women, where she would be safer than in a stranger’s field. So Ruth worked beside them through the barley harvest and then the wheat harvest. At the end of each day, her home was still with Naomi.

## Chapter 3 — At the Threshing Floor

Naomi wanted Ruth to have a home of her own, somewhere she could live securely. “Boaz is our relative,” she said. “Tonight he’ll be winnowing barley at the threshing floor. Wash, put on some oil, and get dressed. Go down there, but stay out of sight while he eats and drinks. Watch where he lies down. Then uncover his feet and lie there. He’ll tell you what to do.”

“I’ll do what you say,” Ruth answered.

At the threshing floor Boaz ate, drank, and went to sleep at the far end of a pile of grain. Ruth came quietly, lifted the cover from his feet, and lay down.

Around midnight he startled awake and turned. A woman was lying at his feet.

“Who are you?”

“It’s Ruth, your servant. Spread the corner of your cloak over me. You’re a close relative with the right to help our family.”

“May the Lord bless you, my daughter. This is an even greater kindness than the one you showed before. You could have gone after younger men, rich or poor. You didn’t. Don’t be afraid. I’ll do what you ask. Everyone in town knows what kind of woman you are.”

There was one thing Ruth still needed to hear. Boaz was a close relative, but another man was more closely related than he was. If that man agreed to take on the family duty, the right was his. If he refused, Boaz swore by the living Lord to take it on himself. For now, Ruth was to stay until morning.

She got up while it was still too dark for people to recognize one another. Boaz wanted her visit kept quiet. He asked her to hold out her cloak, then measured six portions of barley into it and helped her lift the load. He went into town.

“How did it go, my daughter?” Naomi asked when Ruth came home.

Ruth told her everything. She showed Naomi the six measures of barley. Boaz had told her she was not to come home empty-handed.

“Wait here, my daughter,” Naomi said. “We’ll see how it turns out. That man won’t rest until he’s settled it today.”

## Chapter 4 — Before the Witnesses

Boaz went to the town gate and sat down. When the man he had spoken of passed by, Boaz called him over. He asked ten elders of Bethlehem to sit with them. Before those witnesses, he told the man that Naomi had come back from Moab and was selling the piece of land that had belonged to Elimelech.

“You have the first right to redeem it,” Boaz said. “If you mean to buy it, do it here in front of the elders. If you don’t, tell me. I’m next in line after you.”

“I’ll buy it,” the man said.

Boaz explained what came with the land. The buyer would also take Ruth of Moab, Mahlon’s widow, as his wife. The land and the name of the man who had died would stay connected through his family.

“Then I can’t do it,” the man said. “I might put my own inheritance at risk. Take my right to buy it.”

In Israel at that time, a man could seal an agreement about property by taking off his sandal and handing it over. The relative told Boaz to buy the land and took off his sandal.

Boaz turned to the elders and the people at the gate. He called them to witness that he had acquired from Naomi all that had belonged to Elimelech, Chilion, and Mahlon. He would marry Ruth, so Mahlon’s name would remain with his inheritance and live on among his family and in his own town.

“We are witnesses,” the people said. They asked the Lord to make Ruth like Rachel and Leah, the women who built up the family of Israel. They prayed that Boaz would prosper in Ephrathah and be known in Bethlehem, and that the family the Lord gave him through Ruth would be like the family of Perez, the son Tamar bore to Judah.

Ruth and Boaz married. The Lord enabled Ruth to conceive, and she gave birth to a son. The women of Bethlehem blessed the Lord for giving Naomi a relative to carry on the family. They hoped the boy’s name would be known in Israel. He would renew her life, they said, and care for her in old age. And he was the son of Ruth, the daughter-in-law who loved her and had been better to her than seven sons.

Naomi took the child into her arms and cared for him. The neighbors said a son had been born to Naomi. They named him Obed.

From Perez the family line passed through Hezron, Ram, Amminadab, Nahshon, and Salmon to Boaz. Obed, the son of Boaz and Ruth, became the father of Jesse. Jesse became the father of David.
