# Malachi

Editorial manuscript for *The Bible, Retold — A Literary Adaptation for Young English Readers*. The Protestant World English Bible is the public-domain source. Malachi’s question-and-answer disputes, covenant language, difficult marriage passage, economic commands, and closing promise are preserved without using them to settle later interpretive debates. This adaptation is intended to be read alongside Scripture.

## Chapter 1 — What Kind of Gift Is This?

The Lord’s message came to Israel through Malachi.

“I have loved you,” God said.

The people answered with a question: “How have you loved us?”

God pointed to the story of two brothers, Jacob and Esau. Though they belonged to the same family, God had chosen Jacob and set his covenant love upon him. Edom, the nation descended from Esau, had become a wilderness where jackals lived among ruined mountains.

Edom might promise to rebuild whatever had been torn down, but the Lord of Armies declared that he would pull it down again. The land had set itself against him. Israel would see what happened and recognize that the Lord’s greatness reached beyond Israel’s own borders.

Then God spoke to the priests.

A son normally honors his father, and a servant respects his master. If God was their Father, where was his honor? If he was their Master, where was their reverence?

The priests asked how they had despised his name.

The answer was visible on the altar. They offered polluted food while treating the Lord’s table as something unimportant. Blind animals were brought as sacrifices, along with lame and sick animals. The priests gave God gifts they would never dare present to their governor.

Would the governor be pleased? Would he welcome them after receiving such an offering?

Yet they asked God to be gracious. Malachi challenged them to consider whether offerings like these expressed any true desire for his favor.

It would be better, God said, if someone closed the temple doors. Better no fire at all than useless fire on the altar. He took no pleasure in worship that kept the ceremony while withholding honor.

God’s name would be great from sunrise to sunset, honored among the nations with pure offerings. Yet the priests in Jerusalem profaned it. They called the Lord’s table polluted and its food contemptible. They sighed over their work as if worship were only exhaustion. Some brought animals taken by violence; others offered the lame and sick.

Why should the Lord accept such gifts?

A person might own a healthy male animal and promise it to God, then substitute a defective one when the time came to sacrifice. Malachi called that worshiper a deceiver.

God was not a minor ruler who could be dismissed with whatever people did not want. He was a great King, the Lord of Armies, and his name inspired awe among the nations.

The question beneath the sacrifices was simple: if God received only the gift that cost the giver nothing, what did that gift say about the giver’s view of God?

## Chapter 2 — Faithless at the Altar and at Home

The next command was directed to the priests.

If they refused to listen and would not take God’s honor seriously, their blessings would become curses. In fact, the curse had already begun because they had not taken the warning to heart.

Malachi used an image intended to shock them. The waste from their festival sacrifices would be spread across their faces, and they would be carried away with it. Priests who treated holy worship as worthless would share the place of what was thrown out.

Then they would know that God had sent the warning to preserve his covenant with Levi.

That covenant had offered life and peace and called the priest to reverence. A faithful priest stood in awe of God’s name. True teaching filled his mouth; injustice did not stain his speech. He walked with God in peace and integrity and turned many people away from wrongdoing.

A priest’s lips were meant to guard knowledge. People should have been able to seek instruction from him because he served as a messenger of the Lord of Armies.

But these priests had left the path. Their teaching caused others to stumble, and they corrupted the covenant of Levi. Because they showed favoritism when applying the law, God made them despised before the people.

The failure of faithfulness spread beyond the priesthood.

Did the people not share one Father? Had one God not created them? Why, then, did they betray one another and dishonor the covenant of their ancestors?

Judah had acted unfaithfully. Men joined themselves to worshipers of foreign gods and profaned what the Lord loved. Malachi warned that anyone who did this, even while bringing offerings, would be cut off from Jacob’s community.

Another kind of betrayal filled the altar with tears. People wept because God no longer received their offerings with favor, then asked why.

The Lord had witnessed the covenant between a man and the wife of his youth. She was his companion, yet he had treated her treacherously. God had made them one and desired faithful, godly offspring. Therefore each person was to guard the inner life and refuse betrayal.

The passage spoke fiercely about the man who hated and divorced his wife: he covered his clothing with violence. Whatever questions later readers bring to this difficult wording, Malachi’s accusation is clear—the covenant partner must not be discarded through hatred and treachery.

“Guard your spirit,” the Lord warned. “Do not be unfaithful.”

The people had also exhausted God with their words. Again they asked how.

They looked at evil people who seemed successful and concluded either that God approved of evil or that no God of justice would ever appear. Their question named a real ache, but they used it to excuse faithlessness rather than to seek God honestly.

At altar, classroom, community, and home, the same problem kept appearing: people wanted the name of covenant while refusing the faithfulness a covenant required.

## Chapter 3 — The Refiner and the Record

God promised to send a messenger who would prepare the way before him. Then the Lord whom the people claimed to seek would suddenly come to his temple. The messenger of the covenant was coming.

But his arrival would not merely confirm that everything was already right.

Who could endure the day of his coming? Who could stand when he appeared?

He would be like a refiner’s fire and a launderer’s strong soap. A refiner does not destroy silver; he remains beside the heat until impurity separates from what is precious. In the same way, the sons of Levi would be purified like gold and silver so that they could once more bring righteous offerings. Worship from Judah and Jerusalem would again please the Lord as it had in earlier days.

Purification also meant judgment. God would testify swiftly against sorcery, adultery, and false oaths. He would confront those who withheld a worker’s wages, oppressed widows and fatherless children, denied justice to foreigners, and lived without reverence for him.

The Lord had not changed. That was why Jacob’s descendants had not been consumed despite generations of disobedience.

“Return to me,” God said, “and I will return to you.”

The people asked how they were to return.

God answered with another question: could a human being rob God? Yet the whole nation had done so by withholding tithes and offerings. They were to bring the full tithe into the storehouse so that food would be available in God’s house.

In this matter God invited them to test him. Would he not open heaven’s windows and pour out more blessing than they had room to hold? He would restrain what devoured the crops, protect the fields’ fruit, and keep grapes from falling before they ripened. Other nations would call them blessed, and the land would become a place of delight.

Still, harsh words circulated.

The people said there was no value in serving God. What profit came from obeying him or walking in mourning? Proud people appeared happy. Those who practiced evil seemed established; they tested God and escaped.

Not everyone joined that conclusion. Those who feared the Lord spoke with one another. God listened.

A book of remembrance was written before him, recording those who honored his name. They would belong to him as a treasured possession on the day he acted. He would spare them with the tenderness of a father sparing a faithful son.

Then the difference would become visible again: righteous and wicked, the one who served God and the one who did not.

The chapter held two records side by side. One was the public appearance of the moment, where pride seemed profitable. The other was the book before God, where quiet faithfulness was heard, remembered, and called his own.

## Chapter 4 — Before the Great Day

A day was coming, burning like a furnace.

Pride and wickedness would be like dry straw in the heat. The fire would leave neither root below nor branch above—no hidden source from which the old evil could grow again.

But the same coming day carried another image for those who revered God’s name. The sun of righteousness would rise with healing in its wings, spreading light like the rays of dawn.

They would go out with the energy of calves released from a stall, leaping into open ground. Wickedness that had once seemed powerful would be reduced to ashes beneath their feet on the day the Lord acted.

Until then, the people were to remember the law of Moses, God’s servant—the statutes and ordinances commanded at Horeb for all Israel.

They were also to look ahead.

God would send Elijah the prophet before the great and fearful day of the Lord. His work would turn parents’ hearts toward their children and children’s hearts toward their parents. Broken generations would be called back toward one another.

Without that turning, the land faced a curse.

The book ended between memory and expectation: remember Moses; wait for Elijah. Remember the covenant already given; wait for the messenger still to come. The final picture of faithfulness was not only a purified altar or a restored harvest. It was reconciliation inside families before the day of the Lord.
