# Jude

Editorial manuscript for *The Bible, Retold — A Literary Adaptation for Young English Readers*. The Protestant World English Bible is the public-domain source. This letter is retold in accessible English and is intended to be read alongside Scripture.

## Chapter 1 — Guard the Faith, Rescue the Doubting

Jude introduced himself as a servant of Jesus Christ and a brother of James. He wrote to people called by God, loved by the Father, and kept for Jesus Christ. His opening prayer was that mercy, peace, and love would not merely visit them but multiply among them.

He had wanted to write a joyful letter about the salvation they shared. Instead, an urgent danger changed his subject. He appealed to his readers to defend the faith entrusted to God’s people once for all.

Certain people had slipped quietly into the community. Jude described them as ungodly because they twisted God’s grace into permission for immoral behavior and denied Jesus Christ, their only Master and Lord. Their words may have sounded free, but the road they offered led away from the one they claimed to follow.

Jude reminded his readers of judgments they already knew from sacred history. The Lord rescued a people from Egypt, yet those who later refused to believe were destroyed. Angels who abandoned their proper place were kept in darkness for judgment. Sodom, Gomorrah, and the surrounding cities became warnings after pursuing sexual immorality and desires outside God’s order.

The intruders behaved with similar arrogance. They polluted themselves, rejected authority, and insulted glorious beings they did not understand. Jude set their pride beside a different example: even Michael the archangel, while disputing with the devil about Moses’s body, did not pronounce an insulting judgment on his own authority. He said instead, “May the Lord rebuke you.”

These teachers insulted what they did not know and were destroyed by the very instincts they followed without understanding. Jude connected their path with three older rebellions: Cain’s violence, Balaam’s pursuit of profit, and Korah’s revolt against God-given authority.

Then his language became a storm of images. They were hidden reefs at the believers’ shared meals, dangerous beneath the surface. They were shepherds who fed only themselves; clouds that promised rain but held no water; autumn trees without fruit, uprooted and twice dead; wild sea waves throwing up their shame like foam; wandering stars disappearing into deep darkness.

Jude also recalled a prophecy associated with Enoch, the seventh generation from Adam: the Lord would come with countless holy ones to judge ungodly deeds and defiant words. The false teachers complained constantly, followed their desires, boasted loudly, and praised people only when it brought them an advantage.

“But you, beloved,” Jude continued, turning from warning to responsibility, “remember what the apostles of our Lord Jesus Christ told you.” They had warned that mockers would come, controlled by ungodly desires. Such people created divisions. They lived only by natural impulses and did not have the Spirit.

The believers were to choose another way. They were to build one another up in their most holy faith, pray in the Holy Spirit, and keep themselves within the love of God as they waited for the mercy of Jesus Christ leading to eternal life.

Their response to people in danger required wisdom. They were to show mercy to those who doubted. Others needed to be rescued urgently, as if pulled from a fire. Still others required mercy mixed with fear and caution, with hatred for the corruption that had stained their lives. Jude called for compassion without carelessness.

He ended not with confidence in human strength, but with praise to God. God was able to keep his people from stumbling and bring them, without fault and with great joy, into the presence of his glory. To the only God, their Savior through Jesus Christ their Lord, belonged glory, majesty, power, and authority—before all time, now, and forever.

Amen.
