# Philemon

Editorial manuscript for *The Bible, Retold — A Literary Adaptation for Young English Readers*. The Protestant World English Bible is the public-domain source. This letter is retold in accessible English and is intended to be read alongside Scripture.

## Chapter 1 — More Than a Slave

Paul wrote from prison with Timothy beside him. His letter was addressed to Philemon, a dear friend and fellow worker, and also to Apphia, Archippus, and the church that met in Philemon’s house. He began by asking that grace and peace from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ would be with them.

Whenever Paul prayed, he thanked God for Philemon. News had reached him of this man’s faith in Jesus and his love for God’s people. That love had already refreshed many hearts, and Paul prayed that Philemon’s shared faith would lead to a deeper understanding of every good thing believers have in Christ.

Then Paul came to the reason for his letter.

He believed he could speak boldly and tell Philemon what duty required. Instead, he chose to appeal to him through love. Paul was now an old man and a prisoner because of his service to Christ. From his chains, he spoke on behalf of Onesimus, whom he called his child in the faith.

There was pain behind the request. Onesimus had once been useless to Philemon—a word Paul set against the meaning of the man’s name, which suggested usefulness. Now, Paul said, Onesimus had become useful both to Philemon and to Paul.

Paul was sending him back. It felt like sending away part of his own heart.

He would gladly have kept Onesimus close. While Paul remained a prisoner for the good news, Onesimus could have served him in Philemon’s place. But Paul refused to make that decision without Philemon’s agreement. Any kindness had to be freely chosen, not forced.

Perhaps, Paul suggested carefully, their separation had lasted for a short time so that Philemon might receive Onesimus back forever. The relationship could no longer be understood only as one between a master and a slave. Onesimus was now more than a slave: he was a beloved brother. Paul loved him deeply, and Philemon had even more reason to welcome him, both in ordinary life and as a fellow believer in the Lord.

“If you consider me your partner,” Paul urged, “welcome him as you would welcome me.”

If Onesimus had caused any loss or owed anything, Philemon was to charge it to Paul. At that point Paul emphasized that he was writing the promise with his own hand: he would repay the debt. Then he gently reminded Philemon that Philemon himself owed Paul a debt of another kind—his own life in the faith.

Paul asked his brother to give him this joy in the Lord and refresh his heart, just as Philemon had refreshed others. He wrote with confidence, believing Philemon would obey and would probably do even more than Paul requested.

The letter then looked beyond the prison walls. Paul asked Philemon to prepare a guest room, hoping that the prayers of the church would be answered and that he would be restored to them. Epaphras sent greetings, as did Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, and Luke, Paul’s fellow workers.

Paul ended with grace. Yet the appeal remained in Philemon’s hands: he could hold tightly to his legal position, or he could recognize a brother and receive him with love.
