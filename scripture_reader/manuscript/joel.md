# Joel

Editorial manuscript for *The Bible, Retold — A Literary Adaptation for Young English Readers*. The Protestant World English Bible is the public-domain source. Joel’s locust imagery, calls to communal repentance, promise of the Spirit, and judgment poetry are preserved without imposing a disputed modern chronology. This adaptation is intended to be read alongside Scripture.

## Chapter 1 — When Joy Withered

The word of the Lord came to Joel son of Pethuel.

The prophet called first to the elders and then to everyone living in the land. Had they ever seen anything like this? Had their parents known such a disaster? The story must be passed down: parents were to tell their children, those children the next generation, and that generation the one after it.

Wave after wave of insects had consumed the country. What one kind of locust left behind, another ate. What survived that swarm was taken by grasshoppers, and what remained after them was eaten by yet another crawling insect. The exact names changed with each stage; the result did not. Nothing had escaped.

Joel called drinkers to wake and weep because sweet wine had disappeared from their mouths. He described the invading swarm as a nation—strong, beyond counting, armed with a lion’s teeth and a lioness’s fangs. It had destroyed the Lord’s vine and splintered the fig tree, stripping away its bark until the branches shone white.

The land was to mourn like a young bride dressed in rough sackcloth for the husband she had lost.

The disaster reached worship as well as the table. With grain and wine gone, meal offerings and drink offerings could no longer be brought to the Lord’s house. Priests mourned because the fields were ruined. Grain had perished, new wine had dried up, and olive oil had failed.

Farmers stood ashamed before empty ground. Vineyard workers cried over wheat and barley. Vine and fig tree had withered, along with pomegranate, palm, apple, and every other tree of the field.

It seemed that joy itself had dried up among the people.

Joel told the priests to put on sackcloth and spend the night mourning before the altar. They were to set apart a fast, announce a solemn gathering, bring elders and all inhabitants to the Lord’s house, and cry out to God together.

The day of the Lord was near, the prophet warned, coming like destruction from the Almighty. Food had vanished before their eyes, and gladness had left the house of God.

Seeds shriveled beneath hard clods of earth. Storehouses stood empty and barns broke down because no grain remained to fill them. Livestock wandered in confusion without pasture, and sheep suffered with them.

Joel’s public warning became a personal prayer.

“Lord, I cry to you.”

Fire had consumed the wilderness pastures and burned the trees. Even wild animals seemed to pant toward God. Streams were dry, and the places where they once fed had been scorched.

No part of life stood outside the loss: worshipers, priests, farmers, vines, seeds, cattle, sheep, trees, and wild creatures all waited together for mercy.

## Chapter 2 — Return with All Your Heart

A trumpet sounded in Zion. An alarm rang from God’s holy mountain. Everyone in the land had reason to tremble, for the day of the Lord was close.

Joel called it a day of darkness, cloud, and deep shadow. Across the mountains an immense force spread like dawn. Nothing like it had been seen before, and generations to come would remember it.

Fire seemed to move ahead of the army and flame behind it. Before its arrival, the land looked like Eden; afterward, like an empty wilderness. The attackers resembled horses and charged like cavalry. Their movement sounded like chariots leaping over mountain ridges, like fire consuming dry straw, like an army arranged for battle.

People’s faces lost their color. The force ran forward like trained warriors, climbed walls, held its lines, and never pushed another soldier out of place. Defenses could not break its formation. It rushed into the city, crossed walls, climbed into houses, and entered windows like thieves.

Earth and sky trembled before it. Sun, moon, and stars lost their light. Above the noise came the Lord’s voice before his army. His forces were beyond counting, and the one who carried out his command was strong. Who could endure so great and fearful a day?

Then, in the middle of the alarm, came an open door.

“Even now,” the Lord said, “return to me with all your heart.”

The people were to come with fasting, weeping, and grief. But outward signs alone were not enough. They were to tear their hearts, not merely their clothes, and turn to the Lord their God.

Why return? Because he is gracious and compassionate, slow to anger, rich in faithful love, and willing to relent from calamity. Joel did not turn mercy into a formula. “Who knows?” he asked. God might turn, show compassion, and leave enough blessing for grain and drink offerings to return.

The trumpet sounded again, this time to call a fast. Everyone was to gather: elders, children, nursing babies, bridegroom, and bride. Priests were to weep between the temple porch and altar, asking God to spare his people and not let the nations make them an object of shame. Why should outsiders ask, “Where is their God?”

The Lord responded with jealous care for his land and pity for his people. Grain, new wine, and oil would return in abundance. Their disgrace among the nations would end. The northern force would be driven into barren country, between eastern and western seas, where its remains would decay. The enemy had done terrible things, but its terror would not have the final word.

The land was told not to fear. Animals could stop trembling as green pasture returned. Fig tree and vine would bear fruit. The children of Zion could rejoice in the Lord, who would send rain in its proper measure—the early rain and the later rain. Threshing floors would fill with wheat, and vats would overflow with wine and oil.

God promised to restore the years consumed by every wave of locusts. The people would eat until satisfied and praise the Lord who had acted wonderfully. They would know that he was among Israel, that he alone was their God.

Then the promise reached beyond crops.

Afterward, God would pour out his Spirit on all kinds of people. Sons and daughters would prophesy. Old men would dream, and young men would see visions. Even male and female servants—people easily overlooked by society—would receive the Spirit.

Signs would appear in heaven and earth: blood, fire, columns of smoke, darkened sun, and a moon like blood before the great and fearful day of the Lord.

Yet whoever called on the Lord’s name would be saved. Escape would be found in Mount Zion and Jerusalem among the survivors whom the Lord called.

The chapter traveled from an unstoppable devouring army to rain, fruit, a satisfied people, and the Spirit poured out without the usual barriers. Its turning point remained the invitation given while the alarm was still sounding: even now, return with all your heart.

## Chapter 3 — The Lord Dwells in Zion

Joel looked toward a time when the fortunes of Judah and Jerusalem would be restored.

God would gather the nations in the valley of Jehoshaphat—a name connected with the Lord’s judgment. There he would call them to account for their treatment of his people and heritage. Israel had been scattered among the nations, and the land divided. Captives had been treated as objects: lots were cast over them, a boy traded to purchase a prostitute, and a girl sold for enough wine to drink.

Tyre, Sidon, and the regions of Philistia were named. They had taken silver, gold, and precious treasures and carried them into their temples. They had sold children from Judah and Jerusalem to Greeks so that the captives would be taken far from home.

God promised to awaken the exiles in the distant places where they had been sold. The violence of the slave trade would return upon those who had practiced it. Joel described that reversal in the severe legal language of the ancient judgment: the oppressors’ sons and daughters would be sold through Judah to the distant people of Sheba. The prophet presented this as retribution, not as permission for readers to enslave others.

A summons went out to the nations: prepare for war. Wake the warriors. Beat farming tools into weapons—plowshares into swords and pruning hooks into spears. Even the weak would claim strength as the surrounding nations hurried toward the valley.

The prophet called for God’s mighty ones to come down.

The gathered nations entered not as judges, but as those being judged. Harvest imagery replaced military commands. The crop was ripe; the sickle should be used. The winepress and vats overflowed—not with an ordinary harvest, but with the nations’ great wickedness.

Crowds filled the valley of decision. The day of the Lord was near. Sun, moon, and stars darkened.

Then the Lord roared from Zion and thundered from Jerusalem. Heaven and earth shook. Yet for his people, the same Lord was a refuge and stronghold.

They would know that he was their God, dwelling in Zion, his holy mountain. Jerusalem would be holy and no longer crossed by hostile strangers.

The vision changed from judgment to abundance. Mountains dripped sweet wine; hills seemed to flow with milk. Judah’s streams filled with water. A fountain came from the Lord’s house and watered the Valley of Shittim.

Egypt would become desolate and Edom a wilderness because they had shed innocent blood and acted violently against Judah. But Judah would remain inhabited, and Jerusalem continue through generation after generation.

The Lord would deal with bloodguilt that had not yet been cleared.

The final reason for hope was not Judah’s strength, its army, or even its restored fields.

The Lord dwelt in Zion.
